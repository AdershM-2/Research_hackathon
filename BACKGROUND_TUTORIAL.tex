\section{Space Debris Problem Overview}

\subsection{What is Space Debris?}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Defunct satellites}: No longer operational spacecraft
    \item \textbf{Rocket bodies}: Upper stages left in orbit
    \item \textbf{Fragmentation debris}: Pieces from collisions and explosions
    \item \textbf{Mission-related objects}: Lens caps, separation mechanisms, etc.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{LEO Characteristics}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Altitude}: 200--2000 km above Earth
    \item \textbf{Orbital velocity}: $\sim$7.8 km/s (28,000 km/h!)
    \item \textbf{Orbital period}: $\sim$90--120 minutes
    \item \textbf{Atmospheric drag}: Still present, causes gradual decay
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why It Matters}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Over 34,000 tracked objects $>$10 cm
    \item $\sim$1 million objects between 1--10 cm
    \item Collision at orbital speeds can destroy spacecraft
    \item Even small debris (1 cm) can be catastrophic
\end{itemize}

\section{Orbital Mechanics Fundamentals}

\subsection{Kepler's Laws}

As a controls engineer, you need to understand that satellites follow predictable paths:

\subsubsection{Elliptical Orbits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Orbits are ellipses with Earth at one focus
    \item Described by semi-major axis ($a$) and eccentricity ($e$)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Orbital Parameters (State Vector)}

\begin{lstlisting}
Position: [x, y, z]
Velocity: [vx, vy, vz]
\end{lstlisting}

Or using \textbf{Keplerian Elements}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{$a$}: Semi-major axis (orbit size)
    \item \textbf{$e$}: Eccentricity (orbit shape: 0 = circular, $<$1 = ellipse)
    \item \textbf{$i$}: Inclination (tilt relative to equator)
    \item \textbf{$\Omega$}: Right Ascension of Ascending Node (RAAN)
    \item \textbf{$\omega$}: Argument of Periapsis
    \item \textbf{$\nu$}: True Anomaly (position in orbit)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two-Body Problem}

The fundamental equation of orbital motion:

\begin{equation}
\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $\mu = GM$ (gravitational parameter of Earth = 398,600 km$^3$/s$^2$)
    \item $\mathbf{r}$ = position vector
    \item $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}$ = acceleration
\end{itemize}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10!white,colframe=emphcolor,title=Key Insight for Controls]
In orbit, there's no friction! Any velocity change is permanent unless you apply another force.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Hohmann Transfer}

To change orbits efficiently:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply $\Delta v$ at periapsis to raise apoapsis
    \item Coast to new apoapsis
    \item Apply $\Delta v$ to circularize
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Critical for your problem:} Rendezvous requires matching both position AND velocity!

\section{The Kessler Syndrome}

\subsection{The Cascading Collision Problem}

Named after NASA scientist Donald Kessler (1978), this is the critical threat:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Two objects collide at $\sim$15 km/s relative velocity
    \item Collision creates thousands of new debris fragments
    \item Each fragment can cause more collisions
    \item \textbf{Exponential growth} of debris population
    \item Eventually, LEO becomes unusable (chain reaction)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Current Risk Level}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We're approaching the critical density threshold
    \item Some orbits (800--1000 km) are already at risk
    \item Mega-constellations (Starlink: 42,000 satellites planned) increase risk
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Active Removal is Needed}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Natural decay takes decades-to-centuries in LEO
    \item Atmospheric drag only significant below $\sim$600 km
    \item We need to remove $\sim$5--10 large objects per year to stabilize
\end{itemize}

\section{Spacecraft Dynamics \& Control in Space}

\subsection{Attitude Dynamics (Rotation)}

\textbf{Euler's Rotational Equation:}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{I} \cdot \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}) = \boldsymbol{\tau}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $\mathbf{I}$ = Moment of inertia tensor (3$\times$3 matrix)
    \item $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ = Angular velocity vector
    \item $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ = Applied torque
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Attitude Representation:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Euler angles (intuitive but have singularities)
    \item Quaternions (4 parameters, no singularities -- preferred!)
    \item Rotation matrices (9 parameters, redundant)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Actuators in Space}

\subsubsection{For Translation ($\Delta v$):}

\textbf{1. Thrusters}: Chemical or electric propulsion
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Impulse: $\Delta v = v_e \cdot \ln(m_0/m_f)$ -- Tsiolkovsky equation
    \item Fuel limited!
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{For Rotation (torque):}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reaction Wheels}: Spin internal mass, conserve angular momentum
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Fast, precise, can saturate (need desaturation)
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Control Moment Gyroscopes (CMGs)}: Gimbaled spinning wheels
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher torque than reaction wheels
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Magnetic Torquers}: Interact with Earth's magnetic field
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Slow, only certain directions, free propellant
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Thrusters}: Fast but uses fuel
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key Control Challenges}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Underactuated system}: Limited fuel, must be efficient
    \item \textbf{Coupled dynamics}: Translation-rotation coupling during capture
    \item \textbf{Long time delays}: Ground communication $\sim$1 second delay
    \item \textbf{Sensor noise}: Star trackers, gyros, GPS in space
\end{itemize}

\section{Rendezvous and Proximity Operations (RPO)}

\subsection{The Four Phases of Rendezvous}

\textbf{Phase 1: Phasing (Far Range, $>$100 km)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Match orbital plane (inclination)
    \item Adjust orbit to approach target
    \item Use ground-based tracking
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Phase 2: Approach (10--100 km)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Refine trajectory
    \item Begin autonomous sensing
    \item Switch to relative navigation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Phase 3: Proximity Operations (100 m -- 10 km)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Critical phase for your problem!}
    \item Careful trajectory planning
    \item Collision avoidance
    \item Continuous target tracking
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Phase 4: Close Proximity \& Capture ($<$100 m)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Final approach corridor
    \item Synchronize rotation rates
    \item Execute capture
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hill-Clohessy-Wiltshire (HCW) Equations}

\textbf{Relative motion in orbit} (linearized):

\begin{align}
\ddot{x} - 2n\dot{y} - 3n^2x &= \frac{f_x}{m} \\
\ddot{y} + 2n\dot{x} &= \frac{f_y}{m} \\
\ddot{z} + n^2z &= \frac{f_z}{m}
\end{align}

Where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item $(x, y, z)$: Local orbital frame (radial, along-track, cross-track)
    \item $n$: Mean motion (orbital angular velocity) = $\sqrt{\mu/a^3}$
    \item $\mathbf{f}$: Control forces
\end{itemize}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=codeborder,title=Key Insight]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Radial ($x$) and along-track ($y$) are \textbf{coupled} via Coriolis terms ($2n\dot{y}$, $2n\dot{x}$)
    \item Cross-track ($z$) is \textbf{decoupled}
    \item Natural motion creates elliptical relative trajectories
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{V-bar and R-bar Approaches}

\textbf{R-bar (Radial approach):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Approach from above/below
    \item Naturally stable against along-track drift
    \item Requires active station-keeping
\end{itemize}

\textbf{V-bar (Velocity vector approach):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Approach from behind/front
    \item Passive safety (natural separation if engines fail)
    \item Preferred for ISS
\end{itemize}

\section{Capture Mechanisms}

\subsection{Contact-Based Methods}

\subsubsection{1. Robotic Arm}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Example: Canadarm2 on ISS, SSRMS
    \item \textbf{Pros}: Precise, proven technology
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Requires close proximity, complex dynamics
    \item \textbf{Control Challenge}: Free-floating base problem!
\end{itemize}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10!white,colframe=emphcolor,title=Free-Floating Dynamics]
When arm moves $\rightarrow$ spacecraft base reacts (Newton's 3rd law)

You need \textbf{Generalized Jacobian} that accounts for base motion:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Position of end-effector depends on both joint angles AND base motion
    \item Captures induce momentum transfer $\rightarrow$ spacecraft tumbles
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsubsection{2. Harpoon}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Fire penetrating spike into target
    \item Tether connects chaser to target
    \item \textbf{Pros}: Works for non-cooperative targets
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Can fragment target, tether dynamics complex
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Net}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Deploy net to envelop target
    \item \textbf{Pros}: Gentle, encapsulates fragments
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Deployment dynamics, tangling risk
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4. Gripper/Grapple}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Mechanical jaws grip target
    \item Requires attachment point (cooperative targets)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contactless Methods}

\subsubsection{1. Electrostatic Tractor}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Charge spacecraft oppositely $\rightarrow$ Coulomb attraction
    \item \textbf{Pros}: No contact, scalable force
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Requires charge management, works at close range
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{2. Laser Ablation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Vaporize surface material $\rightarrow$ creates thrust on target
    \item \textbf{Pros}: Standoff distance, no rendezvous needed
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Debris from ablation, power requirements
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Ion Beam Shepherd}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Plasma thruster creates force on target
    \item \textbf{Pros}: Gentle, continuous force
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Very slow, complex plasma dynamics
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4. Magnetic Tractor}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Use magnetic fields (for ferromagnetic debris)
    \item \textbf{Pros}: No contact
    \item \textbf{Cons}: Only works for magnetic materials
\end{itemize}

\section{Control Challenges for Non-Cooperative Targets}

\subsection{What Makes a Target ``Non-Cooperative''?}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unknown dynamics}: Tumbling rate unknown
    \item \textbf{No communication}: Can't command it
    \item \textbf{Unknown mass properties}: Center of mass, inertia tensor unknown
    \item \textbf{Unknown shape/structure}: Visual only
    \item \textbf{Unpredictable}: Solar radiation pressure, atmospheric drag variations
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{State Estimation Problem}

You need to estimate:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Position \& velocity} (6 DOF translation)
    \item \textbf{Orientation \& angular velocity} (6 DOF rotation)
    \item \textbf{Total: 12-state estimation problem}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Sensors:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{LIDAR}: Range and range-rate
    \item \textbf{Cameras}: Visual tracking, structure from motion
    \item \textbf{Radar}: All-weather tracking
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Estimation Algorithms:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)
    \item Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) -- better for nonlinear
    \item Particle Filters -- for highly nonlinear/multimodal
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tumbling Dynamics}

Debris often tumbles due to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Collision-induced rotation
    \item Gravity gradient torque
    \item Solar radiation pressure
    \item Magnetic field interaction
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Euler's Equation} (torque-free motion):

\begin{align}
I_1\dot{\omega}_1 &= (I_2 - I_3)\omega_2\omega_3 \\
I_2\dot{\omega}_2 &= (I_3 - I_1)\omega_3\omega_1 \\
I_3\dot{\omega}_3 &= (I_1 - I_2)\omega_1\omega_2
\end{align}

\textbf{Result:} Complex 3D tumbling, periodic for rigid bodies

\subsection{Detumbling Strategies}

\textbf{Option 1: Match Rotation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Estimate target angular velocity
    \item Synchronize chaser rotation
    \item Capture in rotating frame
    \item \textbf{Challenge}: High-rate tumbling $\rightarrow$ high control effort
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Option 2: Active Detumbling Before Capture}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Apply external torque (contactless methods)
    \item Slow down tumbling gradually
    \item Then capture
    \item \textbf{Challenge}: Takes time, requires precise torque control
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Option 3: Capture While Tumbling}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Fast capture mechanism
    \item Accept the momentum transfer
    \item Detumble combined system afterward
    \item \textbf{Challenge}: Huge impact forces, risk of damage
\end{itemize}

\section{Deorbiting Techniques}

\subsection{Natural Decay}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Atmospheric drag gradually lowers orbit
    \item Decay time = $f$(altitude, area-to-mass ratio, solar activity)
    \item \textbf{Problem:} Can take decades above 600 km
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Active Deorbiting}

\subsubsection{1. Direct Re-entry}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Use propulsion to lower periapsis below $\sim$80 km
    \item Atmospheric drag increases exponentially
    \item Object burns up
    \item \textbf{$\Delta v$ required:} $\sim$100--200 m/s from LEO
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{2. Drag Augmentation}

\textbf{a) Drag Sail}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Deploy large surface area (sail)
    \item Increases drag force: $F_{\text{drag}} = 0.5 \rho v^2 C_D A$
    \item Accelerates natural decay
    \item \textbf{Pros:} Passive, no propellant
    \item \textbf{Cons:} Attitude stabilization needed
\end{itemize}

\textbf{b) Inflatable Structures}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Similar to drag sail but lighter
    \item Can increase area by 10--100$\times$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{c) Electrodynamic Tether}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Long conducting wire in Earth's magnetic field
    \item Generates current $\rightarrow$ Lorentz force $\rightarrow$ drag
    \item \textbf{Pros:} No propellant, can generate power
    \item \textbf{Cons:} Complex deployment, tether dynamics
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Graveyard Orbit}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Boost to higher orbit ($>$2000 km)
    \item Out of useful LEO region
    \item \textbf{Problem:} Doesn't solve long-term issue
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Controlled Re-entry}

For large objects ($>$1 ton):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Target specific ocean areas (South Pacific)
    \item Prevent casualty on ground
    \item Requires precise trajectory control
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Differences from Terrestrial Robotics}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No Fixed Base}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Manipulator motion $\rightarrow$ base reaction
        \item Need \textbf{Generalized Jacobian} and \textbf{Dynamic Singularities}
        \item Momentum conservation critical
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{No Gravity (Microgravity)}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Objects don't ``fall''
        \item Small forces persist
        \item Contact forces dominate
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Orbital Dynamics}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reference frame accelerates (centripetal)
        \item Coriolis effects in relative motion
        \item Tidal forces (gravity gradient)
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Propellant Constraints}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Every maneuver costs fuel
        \item Fuel mass = mission lifetime
        \item Optimization critical
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Extreme Environment}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Temperature extremes: $-150^\circ$C to $+150^\circ$C
        \item Radiation damages electronics
        \item Vacuum $\rightarrow$ no convective cooling
        \item Atomic oxygen erodes materials
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Communication Delays}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ground stations: $\sim$1 sec delay
        \item Requires autonomy
        \item Can't teleoperate fast maneuvers
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Sensing Challenges}
    \begin{itemize}

\section{Solved Examples: From Simple to Advanced}

This section contains 6 worked examples to help you understand orbital mechanics and space debris removal concepts. Each example includes a detailed, step-by-step solution explained in simple terms.

\subsection{Example 1: Calculating Orbital Velocity (EASY)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem]
A satellite is orbiting Earth at an altitude of 400 km. What is its orbital velocity?

\textbf{Given:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Altitude: $h = 400$ km
    \item Earth's radius: $R_E = 6371$ km
    \item Gravitational parameter: $\mu = 398600$ km$^3$/s$^2$
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} Think of a satellite like a ball on a string that you're swinging around your head. The faster you swing it, the harder it pulls outward. In space, gravity is like that string pulling the satellite inward. The satellite needs to go just fast enough so that gravity's pull exactly balances its tendency to fly away. Too slow and it falls to Earth; too fast and it escapes into space!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{Step 1:} Calculate orbital radius $r$ (distance from Earth's center)
\begin{equation}
r = R_E + h = 6371 + 400 = 6771 \text{ km}
\end{equation}

\textit{Why?} The satellite isn't orbiting at Earth's surface but 400 km above it. We measure orbits from Earth's center, not the surface, so we add the radius.

\textbf{Step 2:} Use the orbital velocity formula
\begin{equation}
v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{398600}{6771}} = \sqrt{58.85} = 7.67 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3:} Convert to more familiar units
\begin{equation}
v = 7.67 \times 3600 = 27,612 \text{ km/h} \approx 27,600 \text{ km/h}
\end{equation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Answer]
The satellite travels at \textbf{7.67 km/s} or about \textbf{27,600 km/h}! That's about 70 times faster than a commercial airplane. At this speed, the satellite completes one orbit around Earth in about 90 minutes.

\textbf{Reality Check:} The International Space Station orbits at a similar altitude and travels at approximately this speed. You can actually see it passing overhead in the evening!
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Example 2: How Long Does Orbit Take? (EASY-MEDIUM)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem]
Calculate the orbital period (time for one complete orbit) of a GPS satellite at an altitude of 20,200 km.

\textbf{Given:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Altitude: $h = 20,200$ km
    \item Earth's radius: $R_E = 6371$ km
    \item $\mu = 398600$ km$^3$/s$^2$
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} Imagine you're on a merry-go-round. If you stand close to the center, you go around quickly. If you stand at the edge, it takes longer to complete one rotation. Satellites work the same way -- the farther from Earth, the longer one orbit takes!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{Step 1:} Calculate orbital radius
\begin{equation}
r = R_E + h = 6371 + 20200 = 26,571 \text{ km}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2:} Calculate semi-major axis $a$ (for circular orbit, $a = r$)
\begin{equation}
a = 26,571 \text{ km}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3:} Use Kepler's Third Law to find orbital period
\begin{equation}
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{26571^3}{398600}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4:} Calculate step by step
\begin{align}
a^3 &= 26571^3 = 1.876 \times 10^{13} \text{ km}^3 \\
\frac{a^3}{\mu} &= \frac{1.876 \times 10^{13}}{398600} = 4.707 \times 10^7 \text{ s}^2 \\
\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} &= \sqrt{4.707 \times 10^7} = 6859 \text{ s} \\
T &= 2\pi \times 6859 = 43,082 \text{ s}
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 5:} Convert to hours
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{43082}{3600} = 11.97 \text{ hours} \approx 12 \text{ hours}
\end{equation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Answer]
The GPS satellite completes one orbit in \textbf{12 hours} (exactly 11 hours 58 minutes).

\textbf{Cool Fact:} GPS satellites are designed so that exactly 2 complete orbits = 1 Earth day (24 hours). This means they appear in the same position in the sky every day at the same time. That's why your GPS can always find at least 4 satellites overhead!
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Example 3: Hohmann Transfer - Moving Between Orbits (MEDIUM)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem]
A debris removal satellite needs to transfer from a 400 km altitude circular orbit to a 600 km altitude circular orbit to intercept a piece of space junk. Calculate the total $\Delta v$ required for this Hohmann transfer.

\textbf{Given:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Initial orbit: $h_1 = 400$ km, so $r_1 = 6771$ km
    \item Final orbit: $h_2 = 600$ km, so $r_2 = 6971$ km
    \item $\mu = 398600$ km$^3$/s$^2$
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} Imagine you're on a swing. To go higher, you need to push at just the right moment (at the bottom). In space, to change orbits, you fire your rocket engine at specific points. A Hohmann transfer is like taking the most fuel-efficient path -- it's like taking the "economy route" instead of the direct but expensive route!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{Step 1:} Calculate velocities in the initial circular orbit
\begin{equation}
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{398600}{6771}} = 7.67 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2:} Calculate velocities in the final circular orbit
\begin{equation}
v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{398600}{6971}} = 7.56 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\textit{Notice:} The higher orbit is slower! This seems weird but remember -- farther from Earth means weaker gravity pull, so less speed needed to stay in orbit.

\textbf{Step 3:} Calculate semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse
\begin{equation}
a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} = \frac{6771 + 6971}{2} = 6871 \text{ km}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4:} Calculate velocity at periapsis (low point) of transfer orbit using vis-viva equation
\begin{equation}
v_{t1} = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} = \sqrt{398600\left(\frac{2}{6771} - \frac{1}{6871}\right)} = 7.73 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5:} Calculate velocity at apoapsis (high point) of transfer orbit
\begin{equation}
v_{t2} = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r_2} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} = \sqrt{398600\left(\frac{2}{6971} - \frac{1}{6871}\right)} = 7.50 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 6:} Calculate the two $\Delta v$ burns
\begin{align}
\Delta v_1 &= v_{t1} - v_1 = 7.73 - 7.67 = 0.06 \text{ km/s} = 60 \text{ m/s} \\
\Delta v_2 &= v_2 - v_{t2} = 7.56 - 7.50 = 0.06 \text{ km/s} = 60 \text{ m/s}
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 7:} Total $\Delta v$ required
\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{total} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2 = 60 + 60 = 120 \text{ m/s}
\end{equation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Answer]
The total velocity change needed is \textbf{120 m/s}.

\textbf{What this means:} You fire your thrusters for a few seconds at the bottom of your orbit to speed up by 60 m/s, coast for about 45 minutes on the transfer ellipse, then fire again at the top to slow down by 60 m/s.

\textbf{Fuel cost:} Using the rocket equation with typical spacecraft ($v_e \approx 3000$ m/s), this requires about 4\% of your spacecraft's mass in propellant. That's why fuel is precious in space!
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Example 4: Relative Motion - Catching Up to Debris (MEDIUM-HARD)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem]
Your debris removal spacecraft is 100 m behind a tumbling satellite in the same circular orbit at 500 km altitude. If you apply a constant 0.1 N thrust forward for 60 seconds (spacecraft mass = 500 kg), what happens to your relative position?

\textbf{Given:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Initial separation: $y_0 = -100$ m (you're behind)
    \item Thrust: $F = 0.1$ N for $t = 60$ s
    \item Mass: $m = 500$ kg
    \item Orbit radius: $r = 6871$ km
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} This is the weird part of orbital mechanics! In space, if you speed up to catch something in front of you, you actually end up going to a higher orbit and moving SLOWER, so you fall behind! It's like trying to run on a circular treadmill -- pushing forward makes you move outward, not forward. This is why approaching space debris is so tricky!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{Step 1:} Calculate orbital angular velocity $n$
\begin{equation}
n = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r^3}} = \sqrt{\frac{398600}{6871^3}} = 0.001115 \text{ rad/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2:} Calculate acceleration from thrust
\begin{equation}
a_y = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{0.1}{500} = 0.0002 \text{ m/s}^2
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3:} Calculate velocity change during burn
\begin{equation}
\Delta v_y = a_y \times t = 0.0002 \times 60 = 0.012 \text{ m/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4:} Use HCW equations for along-track motion
For constant thrust in the along-track ($y$) direction, the solution is:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = y_0 + \frac{3n a_y}{2n^2}(1 - \cos(nt)) + \frac{\Delta v_y}{n}\sin(nt)
\end{equation}

After the 60 s burn stops, we have:
\begin{align}
\dot{y}(60) &= \Delta v_y = 0.012 \text{ m/s} \\
y(60) &= -100 + \text{(small change during burn)} \approx -99.3 \text{ m}
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 5:} Predict long-term motion (after burn stops)
The HCW equations show that with this velocity, you'll continue in a sinusoidal relative trajectory:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = -99.3 + \frac{0.012}{0.001115}\sin(0.001115 \times t) = -99.3 + 10.8\sin(0.001115t)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 6:} Key insight - radial coupling
Because you thrust along-track, the HCW coupling term ($2n\dot{y}$) causes you to also move radially:
\begin{equation}
x(t) = \frac{2\Delta v_y}{n} = \frac{2 \times 0.012}{0.001115} = 21.5 \text{ m above the target}
\end{equation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Answer]
\textbf{Surprising result:} After your burn:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item You move to an orbit \textbf{21.5 m higher} than the debris
    \item You oscillate forward and backward relative to it with amplitude $\pm$10.8 m
    \item You don't simply move forward -- you create an elliptical relative trajectory!
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What really happened:} By pushing forward, you raised your orbit slightly. Higher orbits are slower, so now you drift backward! The relative motion creates a natural ellipse in the rotating frame.

\textbf{Lesson for debris removal:} To approach from behind, you paradoxically need to thrust \textit{backward} to drop into a lower, faster orbit, then coast up to meet the target. Counter-intuitive, but that's orbital mechanics!
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Example 5: Detumbling a Satellite (HARD)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem]
A defunct satellite (500 mm cube, mass = 50 kg) is tumbling with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = [0.1, 0.05, 0.15]$ rad/s. You want to reduce its tumbling to less than 0.01 rad/s in all axes using an ion beam that can apply 0.001 N·m of torque. How long will it take, and what's the strategy?

\textbf{Given:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Satellite: 0.5 m cube, mass $m = 50$ kg
    \item Initial angular velocity: $\boldsymbol{\omega}_0 = [0.1, 0.05, 0.15]$ rad/s
    \item Target: $|\boldsymbol{\omega}| < 0.01$ rad/s
    \item Available torque: $\tau_{max} = 0.001$ N·m
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} Imagine a spinning ice skater. To stop spinning, they extend their arms (spreads out their mass). For a satellite, we can't change its shape, but we can apply a tiny "brake" force continuously. It's like gradually slowing down a spinning top by gently pressing your finger on it, except in space we use a beam of ions (charged particles) instead of your finger!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{Step 1:} Calculate moment of inertia for a cube
For a uniform cube with side length $L = 0.5$ m:
\begin{equation}
I = \frac{1}{6}mL^2 = \frac{1}{6} \times 50 \times 0.5^2 = 2.08 \text{ kg·m}^2
\end{equation}

Assuming principal axes align with cube edges: $I_1 = I_2 = I_3 = I = 2.08$ kg·m$^2$

\textbf{Step 2:} Calculate initial angular momentum magnitude
\begin{align}
|\mathbf{L}_0| &= I|\boldsymbol{\omega}_0| = I\sqrt{\omega_x^2 + \omega_y^2 + \omega_z^2} \\
&= 2.08\sqrt{0.1^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.15^2} \\
&= 2.08\sqrt{0.0325} = 2.08 \times 0.180 = 0.375 \text{ kg·m}^2\text{/s}
\end{align}

\textbf{Step 3:} Calculate final angular momentum (target state)
\begin{equation}
|\mathbf{L}_f| = I \times 0.01 = 2.08 \times 0.01 = 0.0208 \text{ kg·m}^2\text{/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4:} Angular momentum to be removed
\begin{equation}
\Delta L = |\mathbf{L}_0| - |\mathbf{L}_f| = 0.375 - 0.0208 = 0.354 \text{ kg·m}^2\text{/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5:} Time required with constant torque
Using $\tau = \Delta L / \Delta t$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = \frac{\Delta L}{\tau_{max}} = \frac{0.354}{0.001} = 354 \text{ seconds} \approx 6 \text{ minutes}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 6:} Optimal detumbling strategy

The best approach is to apply torque opposite to the instantaneous angular momentum vector:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}(t) = -\tau_{max} \frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)}{|\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)|}
\end{equation}

This means:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Measure current $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ using cameras/LIDAR
    \item Point ion beam opposite to rotation direction
    \item Update pointing every few seconds as rotation slows
    \item Continue until all components $< 0.01$ rad/s
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step 7:} Energy dissipated
\begin{align}
E_0 &= \frac{1}{2}I|\boldsymbol{\omega}_0|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 2.08 \times 0.180^2 = 0.034 \text{ J} \\
E_f &= \frac{1}{2}I|\boldsymbol{\omega}_f|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 2.08 \times 0.01^2 = 0.0001 \text{ J} \\
\Delta E &= 0.034 - 0.0001 \approx 0.034 \text{ J}
\end{align}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Answer]
\textbf{Time required:} Approximately \textbf{6 minutes} of continuous ion beam operation.

\textbf{Strategy:}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Track satellite rotation using sensors (every 0.5 s)
    \item Compute instantaneous spin axis direction
    \item Point ion beam opposite to spin direction
    \item Apply maximum torque (0.001 N·m)
    \item Update beam direction as satellite slows
    \item Stop when all rotation rates $< 0.01$ rad/s
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Challenges:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Must continuously track a tumbling object
    \item Ion beam pointing must be very accurate ($< 1°$)
    \item Need to avoid creating new tumbling modes
    \item Must maintain safe distance (5-10 m) during operation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Real-world note:} This is actually optimistic! Real debris might have unknown mass distribution, making $I$ uncertain. Also, solar radiation pressure and gravity gradients can disturb the process. Actual detumbling missions might take 10-15 minutes.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Example 6: Complete ADR Mission Planning (VERY HARD)}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=green!5!white,colframe=green!60!black,title=Problem - Multi-Part Mission}
Plan a complete Active Debris Removal (ADR) mission:

\textbf{Initial State:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Your chaser spacecraft: 400 km altitude circular orbit, inclination = 51.6°
    \item Debris (500 mm cube): 600 km altitude circular orbit, inclination = 51.6°, tumbling at 0.12 rad/s
    \item Chaser mass: 800 kg, fuel: 200 kg, $I_{sp} = 300$ s (chemical thruster)
    \item Time constraint: Complete mission in 48 hours
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mission phases:}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Hohmann transfer to debris altitude
    \item Approach to 50 m distance
    \item Detumble debris
    \item Capture using robotic arm
    \item Deorbit to 250 km altitude (burns up in weeks)
\end{enumerate}

Calculate total $\Delta v$ budget and verify mission feasibility.
\end{tcolorbox}

\textbf{ELI10 Concept:} This is like planning a complete rescue mission in space! You need to: (1) drive your spaceship to the right highway (transfer orbits), (2) carefully approach the broken satellite (proximity operations), (3) stop it from spinning (detumbling), (4) grab it with a robot arm (capture), and (5) push it down toward Earth where it'll burn up safely (deorbit). Every step needs careful planning because you have limited fuel and time!

\textbf{Solution:}

\textbf{PHASE 1: Hohmann Transfer (400 km $\rightarrow$ 600 km)}

From Example 3, we know:
\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{transfer} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2 = 120 \text{ m/s}
\end{equation}

Transfer time (half orbital period of transfer ellipse):
\begin{align}
a_t &= \frac{6771 + 6971}{2} = 6871 \text{ km} \\
T_t &= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a_t^3}{\mu}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{6871^3}{398600}} = 5525 \text{ s} \\
t_{transfer} &= \frac{T_t}{2} = 2763 \text{ s} \approx 46 \text{ minutes}
\end{align}

\textbf{PHASE 2: Approach from 10 km to 50 m}

Starting separation: 10 km along-track (phasing error after transfer)

Use V-bar approach (safer): Multiple small burns over several orbits
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Small $\Delta v$ to drop slightly below target: 2 m/s
    \item Coast for 2-3 orbits to catch up: 3 hours
    \item Small $\Delta v$ to raise back to target: 2 m/s
    \item Final approach burns (several iterations): 6 m/s
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{approach} \approx 10 \text{ m/s}, \quad t_{approach} \approx 6 \text{ hours}
\end{equation}

\textbf{PHASE 3: Detumbling}

From Example 5, with better thrusters:
\begin{equation}
t_{detumble} \approx 15 \text{ minutes}
\end{equation}

Station-keeping during detumbling (maintaining 50 m distance):
\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{station} \approx 5 \text{ m/s}, \quad t_{detumble} = 15 \text{ min}
\end{equation}

\textbf{PHASE 4: Capture}

Final approach (50 m $\rightarrow$ contact):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Slow, controlled approach: 0.1 m/s approach velocity
    \item Multiple correction burns: 3 m/s total
    \item Time: 10 minutes
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{capture} \approx 3 \text{ m/s}, \quad t_{capture} = 10 \text{ min}
\end{equation}

\textbf{PHASE 5: Deorbit (600 km $\rightarrow$ 250 km)}

Combined mass: $m_{total} = 800 + 50 = 850$ kg

Calculate $\Delta v$ for deorbit burn:
\begin{align}
r_1 &= 6971 \text{ km (current orbit)} \\
r_2 &= 6621 \text{ km (target perigee)} \\
a_d &= \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} = 6796 \text{ km}
\end{align}

Velocity at 600 km altitude:
\begin{equation}
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{398600}{6971}} = 7.56 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

Velocity needed for deorbit ellipse at periapsis:
\begin{equation}
v_{d} = \sqrt{398600\left(\frac{2}{6971} - \frac{1}{6796}\right)} = 7.42 \text{ km/s}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\Delta v_{deorbit} = v_1 - v_{d} = 7.56 - 7.42 = 0.14 \text{ km/s} = 140 \text{ m/s}
\end{equation}

\textbf{TOTAL MISSION BUDGET}

\begin{align}
\Delta v_{total} &= \Delta v_{transfer} + \Delta v_{approach} + \Delta v_{station} + \Delta v_{capture} + \Delta v_{deorbit} \\
&= 120 + 10 + 5 + 3 + 140 \\
&= 278 \text{ m/s}
\end{align}

\textbf{Fuel Requirement Check}

Using Tsiolkovsky equation:
\begin{equation}
\Delta v = v_e \ln\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right) = g \cdot I_{sp} \cdot \ln\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)
\end{equation}

where $v_e = g \cdot I_{sp} = 9.81 \times 300 = 2943$ m/s

After capturing debris, total mass = 850 kg, including remaining fuel.

Required mass ratio:
\begin{equation}
\frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{\Delta v / v_e} = e^{278/2943} = e^{0.0944} = 1.099
\end{equation}

Fuel needed for deorbit:
\begin{equation}
m_{fuel,deorbit} = m_{total}(1.099 - 1) / 1.099 = 850 \times 0.090 = 76.5 \text{ kg}
\end{equation}

For all mission phases (working backwards):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Initial mass: 800 kg
    \item Fuel available: 200 kg
    \item Fuel for transfer + approach: $\sim$40 kg
    \item Remaining after capture: $\sim$160 kg
    \item Fuel needed for deorbit: 76.5 kg
    \item \textbf{Margin: $\sim$80 kg (40\%)} $\checkmark$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Timeline Check}

\begin{align}
t_{total} &= t_{transfer} + t_{approach} + t_{detumble} + t_{capture} + t_{deorbit} \\
&= 46\text{ min} + 6\text{ hr} + 15\text{ min} + 10\text{ min} + 5\text{ min} \\
&\approx 7.5 \text{ hours}
\end{align}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!60!black,title=Final Answer - Mission Feasibility]
\textbf{Mission: FEASIBLE} $\checkmark$

\textbf{Summary:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Total $\Delta v$ required: \textbf{278 m/s}
    \item Fuel needed: \textbf{$\sim$120 kg} (60\% of available)
    \item Safety margin: \textbf{40\%} (good for uncertainties)
    \item Total mission time: \textbf{7.5 hours} (well within 48 hr limit)
    \item Debris disposal: Perigee at 250 km $\rightarrow$ natural decay in 2-4 weeks
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Critical Success Factors:}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item Accurate orbit determination ($< 100$ m error)
    \item Successful detumbling (otherwise capture fails)
    \item Robust control during capture (free-floating base dynamics)
    \item Contingency fuel reserve for emergencies (40\% margin)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Risk Factors:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Debris might fragment during capture (use gentle approach)
    \item Solar radiation pressure affects trajectory ($\sim$1 m/s over 48 hr)
    \item Sensor failures require backup navigation
    \item Combined system might be unstable after capture (need active control)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why this works:} The 40\% fuel margin gives us room for:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Unexpected maneuvers during approach
    \item Longer detumbling time if debris is worse than expected
    \item Multiple capture attempts if first one fails
    \item Emergency abort back to safe orbit
\end{itemize}

This is a realistic ADR mission profile similar to those being planned by ESA's ClearSpace-1 and other upcoming debris removal missions!
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Key Equations Cheat Sheet}

\subsection{Orbital Mechanics}

\begin{align}
\text{Orbital velocity:} \quad v &= \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}} \\
\text{Orbital period:} \quad T &= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} \\
\text{Vis-viva equation:} \quad v^2 &= \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) \\
\text{Hohmann } \Delta v: \quad \Delta v_1 &= \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}} - 1\right)
\end{align}

\subsection{Relative Motion (HCW)}

\begin{align}
\ddot{x} - 2n\dot{y} - 3n^2x &= \frac{f_x}{m} \\
\ddot{y} + 2n\dot{x} &= \frac{f_y}{m} \\
\ddot{z} + n^2z &= \frac{f_z}{m}
\end{align}

where $n = \sqrt{\mu/a^3}$

\subsection{Attitude Dynamics}

\begin{align}
\mathbf{I} \cdot \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}) &= \boldsymbol{\tau} \\
\text{Quaternion kinematics:} \quad \dot{\mathbf{q}} &= 0.5 \, \Omega(\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf{q}
\end{align}

\subsection{Drag Force}

\begin{equation}
F_{\text{drag}} = 0.5 \rho v^2 C_D A
\end{equation}

where $\rho$ = atmospheric density

\subsection{Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation}

\begin{equation}
\Delta v = v_e \ln\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)
\end{equation}

where $v_e$ = exhaust velocity
